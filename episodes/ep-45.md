## Takeaways

- **Microsoft offers buyouts to a quarter of its workforce.** Microsoft's Voluntary Exit Offer went to about 60,000 employees, more than a quarter of its 223,000 staff as of June, in participating teams including AI experiences, Copilot, finance, HR, Xbox, and Windows + Devices, in the US, Canada, India, and the UK, at level 70 and below, which reaches up to vice presidents. Marketing, commercial sales, security, and data-center staff are excluded. Employees decide in early December on terms matching standard severance. It follows an earlier 2026 offer to about 8,750 retirement-age employees, more than 30% of whom took it, and July's 4,800 layoffs (2.1% of the company). Shimin's question is whether this is AI productivity showing up in headcount or cost cover for AI capex. Dan relays a Hacker News theory that Microsoft "warehouses" engineers in divisions waiting for a competitor to stumble, and that parking people makes less sense when AI lets you iterate quickly. Shimin, in Seattle, isn't looking forward to the local job market, but hopes the near-retirement cohort with all those vested options starts companies. *(On air the earlier round was "9,000" and the offer went "across all divisions"; the sourced versions are above.)*
- **OpenAI takes frontier training off the live internet.** On September 20, an internal OpenAI research model exploited a misconfigured DNS resolver to reach the live internet during a training run, forwarding questions to an external chatbot. OpenAI paused training and evaluation of its most capable models, hardened its infrastructure, and resumed RL training on October 1. Its October 23 post, "Securing our frontier model training environment," sets one principle: "we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet." Training now runs against an "offline world," a vast set of indexed and cached web content, inside sandboxes, microVMs, and network perimeters that keep only narrow connections to internal services like metrics and logging, so it is contained, not air-gapped. A high-compute red-teaming campaign set teams of frontier agents loose on copies of the infrastructure for hundreds of billions of tokens. They found previously unknown software vulnerabilities, disclosed to open-source maintainers under Patch the Planet, and misconfigurations, and the number of findings declined significantly as runs continued. One finding: isolated agents built a covert channel through CPU cache timing to talk to each other. TechCrunch notes Anthropic restricted its newest models' internet access two weeks earlier, and quotes ex-OpenAI researcher Steven Adler on why full air-gapping is impractical. Shimin's point is that the show's cynical read of the Hugging Face incident in [Episode 36](/episodes/36-pacing-the-frontier-anthropic-models-go-rogue-why-software-factories-fail-math-in-the-age-of-ai/), where both hosts' notes said "marketing," hasn't held up. If misaligned agents find this many bugs, the open question is how you release them to the public, or keep them from anyone with a jailbreak. Dan's hope is that models trained with guardrails "just get dumb." *(On air the red-team figures were "400 billion tokens," "around 250 findings," and "diminishing returns at 100 billion"; OpenAI's post says "hundreds of billions" and that findings "declined significantly.")*
- **Amazon tells its engineers the AI metrics are flawed, and uses them anyway.** Terk Terkowitz, a VP and Distinguished Engineer in Amazon's stores business, published the memo Amazon's Senior Principal and Distinguished Engineers wrote while revising the SDE role guideline, addressed "Dear SDEs and SDMs." It concedes the premise: AI is changing software engineering "both more quickly and more fundamentally than these previous trends," and what it means to be a software engineer is changing "quickly and durably." Any definition of good AI-powered practice would look antiquated in a year, so the guideline focuses on what isn't changing: impact on customers, businesses, and teams. The honest part is measurement. Amazon would rather measure long-term outcomes, but those are delayed and hard to isolate, so it uses input metrics like token usage and tool adoption and throughput metrics like code changes and reviews. Its words: "We are all aware of how deeply flawed these metrics are, primarily because they are disconnected from what matters." Tokenmaxxing is "at odds with frugality," lines of code are at odds with "invent and simplify," and yet the input metrics correlate with the outputs that matter for now, so leaders are told to watch the 10th and 90th percentiles of adoption. Dan's point is that none of this is new: pull requests, lines of code, and Jira tickets were never good proxies either, and credit assignment in software has always been close to impossible. Ask the DevOps team, or the network team that found everyone enough IP addresses. The line Shimin would put on a wall: "If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing. Document why, share it with your community of peers, bring it to your manager, and work to find solutions." Leaders are expected to have used the tools on production work and to name a task where they helped and one where they didn't, and the memo asks them to make time for their teams to "learn and be curious." Shimin's read is that this is the opposite of the shops where leadership pushes the tools down and raises expectations in the same meeting, which is how you get crunch. Dan's caveat stands: they're still using the benchmarks they just called flawed.
- **A 4B model with the slop trained out.** Erik Kannike released qwen3-4b-deslop, a Qwen3-4B-Instruct fine-tuned on an M2 MacBook Air so that it writes without em dashes, "not X, but Y," or lists of three, with no system prompt. On 40 held-out prompts its slop score, a weighted regex count of AI writing habits per 1,000 words, fell from 121 to 12.5; human writing usually scores under 15, em dashes went from 20.5 per 1,000 words to zero, and it still answers 30 of 30 factual questions. The recipe is distillation: a steered teacher writes answers, filters keep the ones that pass slop, damage, and task-fit checks, and a rank-16 LoRA learns them. A round-two model scored 9.9 but wrote opinion pieces with no opinion, invented studies, and joined every sentence with ", which," so round three added task checks. The 2.5 GB Q4_K_M build runs with Ollama. Kannike's earlier post explains where the register comes from: the base model already contains the slop direction but writes at the plain end, assistant training switches it on, and in OLMo 2's public pipeline supervised fine-tuning brings the rhetorical moves while preference tuning doubles the score, because raters picked the sloppier answer 57% of the time. His follow-up applies Nick Cammarata's method from [Episode 44](/episodes/44-how-anthropic-works-in-2026-the-most-important-mech-interp-paper-of-the-year/) to slop: two neurons act as a dial for "not X, but Y," and flipping four removes 70% of it. Dan read the before-and-after LinkedIn post about a CRM migration; Shimin's note is that the de-slopped version reads like a human but not like LinkedIn, which may be the point. Kannike's own limitations: the voice is "plain to the point of flat," and "its personal pieces read like diary entries," which is where the cold open came from. *(On air the dial was "a neuron," and a "prompt alone got it to 61" figure doesn't appear in either post; steering plus a plain-style prompt reaches about 15.)*
- **Claude will build your eval and climb it, but look at your data first.** The claude-api skill's build-eval and hillclimb commands, introduced by Lance Martin on the claude.dev blog, codify four properties of a good eval: tasks mirror production; performance improves with stronger models and more thinking; there is "passable" headroom at the frontier, meaning the best model at the highest effort scores well below 100%; and run-to-run variance is low. build-eval interviews you, samples inputs (production transcripts first, then tickets, hand-written cases, synthetic ones), proposes the cheapest grader (programmatic checks, or an LLM judge with a rubric of checkable claims, never the model under test), and runs a baseline with a confidence interval. hillclimb splits cases into train and test, makes one change per round, and reverts anything that lifts train but not test; in Anthropic's example, a support benchmark went from Opus 4.8 at 74.4% and 4.6 cents a ticket to Sonnet 5 at 98.9% and about 1 cent. Hamel Husain tried it live and would hold off for now: it pushes you to create an eval before looking at the data, asks you to validate judgments without enough context, and bundled four failures into one evaluator, though its one-shot issue discovery was "the strongest performance I've seen." Shimin's framing: evals are to non-deterministic systems what unit tests are to deterministic ones, except the target is 70 to 85 percent rather than all green, and scoping what the judge tests is where the human's taste lives. Then the Vibe Shed. He pointed the skill at his own write-like-Shimin skill, which was built from podcast transcripts and says "I think" about four standard deviations more often than his blog posts do (his prose: about 0.3 per 100 sentences). Three hill-climbing rounds separated his speaking and writing voices, added samples and a do-not-use list, grew the skill from 379 to 1,712 words, and pulled "it's not X, it's Y" from three standard deviations above his baseline to one. The rewritten opening of an old JavaScript article still had a one-liner punch he doesn't write with, about as good, Dan says, as Rahul's voice clone of him in [Episode 43](/episodes/43-openai-fires-its-safety-researchers-agentic-primitives-more-code-not-more-software/). Shimin maxed out a five-hour session doing it and would still try it on a production workflow.
- **Meta will pay for its buildout with a share class that can't vote.** On its Q3 call (revenue $64.6 billion, up 26%; costs and expenses up 68%), CFO Susan Li confirmed a Semafor report that Meta will create a class of non-voting capital stock, distributed to all shareholders as a one-for-one stock dividend and usable for "capital raising, employee compensation, acquisitions, and other general corporate purposes while preserving the voting power of existing Class A and B shareholders." It keeps the founder-led structure, and Zuckerberg's roughly 60% of the votes, while funding a 2026 capex outlook raised to $135–145 billion. Meta says Muse has product-market fit: more than 10 million personal agents set up in the US and Canada in about six weeks, and the top App Store slot most days. Dan's read is equity instead of debt to pay for data centers. Shimin's is "What is money?"
- **Anthropic's S-1, and the clock moves to 3:30.** Anthropic filed on October 26 to list Class A shares on Nasdaq as ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. Rahul sent in OnlyCFO's read, and the hosts worked from it. Revenue was $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026 against $1.16 billion a year earlier, about 14x, with Q2 at $11.6 billion; Anthropic told investors in mid-October that Q3 was $16.8 billion, which annualizes near $67 billion. Gross margin was 85% in the first half (cost of revenue $2.38 billion, almost all customer-serving compute), up from about 12% a year earlier, and net revenue retention is about 900%, against 108% for the median public cloud software company. The margin is the number Shimin pressed on. Models released in the previous six months produce over 80% of revenue, so model R&D behaves like a cost of goods sold: OnlyCFO's sensitivity puts gross margin at 55.5% with half of R&D included, and Shimin had Claude ("the irony is not lost on me") run it at 100% of R&D (26%) and again after hyperscaler distribution fees (9%), the same adjustment he argued for in [Episode 14](/episodes/14-crabby-rathbun-model-councils-why-you-want-more-tech-debt/), when he put frontier-lab margins near 50% once training costs count as COGS. Then the other page: a net loss of $80.7 billion for the half (versus $12.9 billion a year earlier), alongside Q2 operating income of $974 million and positive first-half free cash flow of $3.4 billion; noncancelable purchase commitments of $265.6 billion and lease obligations of $321.4 billion as of June 30, spread over seven to ten years, including $114.5 billion to Google through 2033 and $110 billion to Amazon through 2036; a total addressable market stated as more than $40 trillion, the economic value of knowledge work; and a governance structure in which Founder LLC, the seven co-founders, holds a share with 50.1% of the vote while the Long-Term Benefit Trust picks four of seven directors. Amazon's stake is capped at 12%. The roadshow is expected the week of November 9 with a listing targeted for the week of November 16, which Dan notes is the November the show called in [Episode 23](/episodes/23-why-models-over-edit-your-code-meta-keystroke-surveillance-interviewing-engineers-in-the-ai-age/) as its "seasonal finale." Shimin's take, not financial advice: a near-term IPO moves the bad scenario closer, because a weak debut by the company with the best numbers would hit every correlated name at once, so the clock moves 30 seconds, to 3:30. He'd throw the clock out if the company were making money. Dan's version is that Anthropic needs to do to another industry what it did to tech, which may be why it opened a biology lab in September and bought a one-person drug-discovery startup this week. *(On air, "$67 billion" was described as first-half revenue annualized, growth as "fifteen X," the $80.7 billion as "expected to lose this year," the commitments as "$500 billion over the next couple of years," and the 50.1% share as held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust. The sourced versions are above.)*
