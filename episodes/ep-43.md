Shimin, Dan, and Rahul open with OpenAI firing three safety researchers, Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni. The three say the outside work at issue was the investigation into the Hugging Face intrusion that [Episode 36](/episodes/36-pacing-the-frontier-anthropic-models-go-rogue-why-software-factories-fail-math-in-the-age-of-ai/) covered. Then come two follow-ups to that episode's Pacing the Frontier statement: Satya Nadella's case for treating models as insider risks, and Mark Zuckerberg saying Meta has reached the frontier and won't slow down. In the Tool Shed, Rahul walks through Charlie Guo's "Agentic Primitives 101." Shimin runs Gemini 4 Argon through the SVG Hamlet test from [Episode 41](/episodes/41-meta-muses-0-day-dhhs-rails-world-keynote-gpt-6-astra-the-llmentalist-effect/) and his second-order-effects prompt. Rahul's first Vibe and Tell is a surprise: he joins the call speaking as Dan, through a voice clone he vibe-coded. Post Processing covers a Harvard study of Jellyfish data, which finds AI coding agents produce more code but not more software. Two Minutes to Midnight reads Bain's $6 trillion revenue estimate, Character.AI's bankruptcy, and a report that OpenAI's revenue is $20 billion below earlier projections. The clock holds at 4:15.

## Takeaways

- **OpenAI's firings test whether safety work can involve outsiders.** OpenAI says Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni violated policies on "accessing and handling sensitive company information," and a spokesperson cites a "pattern of misconduct." Wang says she was told it was for opening an executive's email, access she says had been delegated to her for recruiting. The three answer in an open letter: "AI is not a normal technology, and OpenAI is not a normal company." The work at issue, they say, was the investigation into the Hugging Face intrusion. Korbak was METR's technical point of contact for it. They warn the firings have made former colleagues afraid to work with outside experts. They also deny leaking to The Information about a less monitorable "recurrent depth" architecture, which the show discussed as looped transformers in Episode 41. Shimin had read the unusually detailed third-party write-up of the incident as a win for transparency. Now he wonders whether that access was ever meant to happen. *(On air we said an internal memo tied the firings to the leak. The memo TechCrunch quotes says the decisions "were not about raising safety concerns.")*
- **Two more frontier labs won't pace the frontier.** Nadella's "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era" treats models like employees who might go bad. His controls: observe everything, verify, keep independent controls and audits, contain, and disclose incidents. Shimin's favorite line is about model diversity: "No one model should become the sole dependency for an important outcome or be responsible for verifying its own work." Nadella stops well short of asking anyone to slow down. Zuckerberg, per the FT, says Meta has reached the frontier and rejects calls for a slowdown. AI, he says, "can be engineered with normal guardrails," and he rejects "the anthropomorphic vision of AI, which is that it is just going to go do its own thing." Shimin's read: pacing the frontier is a collective action problem, the current US government won't impose it, and the defectors are already here. Dan notes that meanwhile, Anthropic is worried AI will run itself on five smart TVs. Shimin also owes Meta an apology for mocking its puzzle-solving engineers. Muse Wonder Full scores 56 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, level with Sonnet 5.5 and behind Opus 5.5's 58. *(The API model, Muse Wonder, scores 52; on air we gave it the 56.)*
- **An agent harness has three jobs: doing the work, continuing it, and delegating it.** Charlie Guo's "Agentic Primitives 101" sorts this year's agent machinery into three buckets. Doing the work: system instructions, skills loaded on demand, tools and connectors, sandboxes. Continuing it: sessions and compaction, schedules and events. Delegating it: subagents. Rahul rarely compacts anymore. OMP, the harness from [Episode 34](/episodes/34-apple-sues-openai-boko-haram-s-frontier-ai-state-of-cli-coding-agents-global-workspace-in-llms/), has a `/shake` command that drops tool-call output and can cut context by 20% or more. Shimin's complaint is that cron is dumb. An agent should decide when it next wakes up, or wake on an event like a new email. Guo's emerging patterns are memory (markdown files work for now), proactivity, and identity. On identity, Guo prefers delegation, where the agent holds its own credentials, over impersonation for most tasks. Rahul warns that without proper identity, actions taken in ephemeral sandboxes will be untraceable, a legal and governance problem waiting to happen. Platforms are already picking sides: JPMorgan Chase now blocks outside agents like Muse from directly accessing customer accounts. Rahul expects agent-first banks and stores. Shimin expects agents to come with an org chart, middle management included. Guo also reports, citing OpenAI, that the Millennium Prize result from Episode 41 came from a swarm of about 10,000 agents.
- **Gemini 4 Argon over-delivers, and the frontier models are converging.** Argon ties GPT-6 Astra Max at 53 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Shimin gave it the same one-line prompt as Astra, "create an SVG Shakespeare," and it built an app. It has a stage, a sidebar of lines tagged with emotional beats, lip sync, eyes that follow the cursor, a soundtrack, and a facial-rig inspector with sliders down to "tear glisten." On his second-order-effects prompt, Shimin finds Argon hard to tell apart from Astra, Fable, and Opus 5.5 on quality. Its quirks: it keeps returning to what it means to be human, and it proactively spins up a subagent to build explanatory diagrams. It shows its math, and the math checks out: 98% reliability per step over 30 steps is about 54% end to end. It predicts code review moves to adversarial panels of agents, and proposes "biodiversity mandates" for AI that echo Nadella. Its long-run forecast: not a smooth transition for the next 10 to 20 years, but a golden age for "high-agency misfits." It also gave Rahul his cold-open nickname: Homo Ludens, the playing man. Rahul would rather the show had reviewed Haiku 5.5, which scores 43, near Kimi K3.
- **Cloning a voice is now a hobby project.** For his first Vibe and Tell, Rahul left the call and came back as Dan. He vibe-coded the whole pipeline. He isolated Dan's voice from the show's recordings, stripped speaker identity with ContentVec, and shifted his own pitch down about six semitones. Then he routed the result through the BlackHole virtual audio device into the recording software. His wife heard it on a Google Meet and said it sounded just like Dan. Dan heard one of his own vocal tics, exaggerated. Shimin heard uncanny valley with flashes of the real Dan. For Rahul, the strange part was hearing his own speech patterns in someone else's voice. Rahul has no audio-engineering background, and he points out that a voicemail greeting can be enough audio to clone someone. A motivated scammer will do better than he did. Dan's defense: agree on a code word with family, in person, before anyone needs it.
- **AI coding agents produce more code, not more software.** Fiona Chen and James Stratton (Harvard) studied Jellyfish data from 718 firms and about 300 million work events. In the 12 months after firms adopted coding agents, lines of code rose 30%, commits 20%, and pull requests 23%. Jira issues resolved per worker rose about 3%. That isn't statistically significant, and the confidence interval rules out more than about 12%. The extra work piled up in review. Review time rose 49%, comments per PR rose 35%, and the share of workers doing reviews rose 14%. Change requests grew with time since adoption and were 21 points above a 13% baseline by month 12. Dan finds that heartening: someone is calling out the slop. Per Ars Technica, 80% of firms use AI code review, yet AI writes only 23% of review comments. Rahul expects the bottleneck to clear as agents take over review. He also predicts a long tail of bespoke software, built for ten users who pay enough to make it worth vibe coding. Dan expects operations to be the next bottleneck: who maintains 350,000 custom copies of software, and who changes the about page? Shimin notes that most developer work is translating business requirements into code, and asks how long a human intermediary adds value. Dan's answer: today, because he can fix what non-technical people can't, even with Claude. In a year, probably not.
- **Bain's $6 trillion is mostly new products, and the clock holds at 4:15.** Bain says AI needs $6T in annual revenue by 2031 to justify the data center build-out. That's a sum: $4.2T from new product development (search, advertising, autonomy, physical AI), $1–1.4T from enterprise productivity, and $200–400B from consumers. Annual AI infrastructure spend could reach $1.5T by 2031. Epoch AI's figures for Meta's Prometheus campus show the scale: 600 MW at $24B in 2025, up to 2 GW at $80B by 2027, and 9 GW at $200B by 2030. Dan still can't get over compute being measured in gigawatts. Shimin asks where the power will come from when gas turbines are back-ordered for years. Rahul offers space data centers. Dan objects to shooting GPUs into orbit with no way to get them back. Character.AI became a unicorn about a year after its 2021 founding, and filed for Chapter 11 on October 12. Google's $2.7B licensing deal in 2024 brought Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas back to Google. Shazeer left for OpenAI this June, and Disney paid $20M for some of the assets. Per the FT, OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue is approaching $50B, not the roughly $70B reported earlier. That number came from OpenAI's own investors, trying to compare it with Anthropic, which counts revenue from cloud partners that OpenAI doesn't. The hosts see storm clouds but no lightning, so the clock holds at 4:15. *(On air we called the $4.2T a gap, called the $1.5T current capex, attributed the data center figures to the largest single data center, and said $2.4B. The sourced figures are above.)*
