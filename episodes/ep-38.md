Guest co-host Nick Muy — CISO and VP of Platform Engineering at strut.io, previously security engineering at Expedia Group and cybersecurity strategy at the US Department of Homeland Security — takes the third chair for a week that keeps circling one question: what is AI quietly doing to the people who use it? The News covers a 26,000-student SSRN study in which six-plus months of AI homework help cut Gao Kao scores 18–24% — except for the "AI-augmented" students who kept their study hours and lost nothing — and GitHub's commit volume doubling in four months, incident report included. The Sit Down works through Nick's "Builders Gonna Build" thesis: tech was never the bottleneck, orchestrating agent fleets is middle management whether or not you asked for it, and nobody has an answer for codebases growing ten million lines a week. Vibe and Tell is Shimin's own experiment: 1,000 questions across four 2026 models finds statistically significant AI sandbagging — worse answers for users the model judges unable to verify them, including three of four models hiding the debt-snowball method from "Rhonda." And Two Minutes to Midnight holds at four as Stripe buys OpenRouter for $7.5B on a leaked singularity memo and the FT pegs Anthropic's flagship at ~11% of usage against a rumored $2T IPO.

## Takeaways

- **The AI homework damage tracked the time saved, not the tool used.** Across 26,000 Chinese students, six-plus months of AI homework help dropped Gao Kao scores 18–24% — "the difference between Harvard and your local community college" — while homework time fell from 64 to 45 minutes. The group that used AI but kept spending the same time showed no penalty at all. The hours you stop spending were the thing doing the teaching. The hosts' extension: grueling early-career work is where developers find their own taste — and your next 2am sev is the exam.
- **GitHub's commit doubling is a load story more than a productivity story.** Commit volume doubled in four months as AI agents pushed the cost of producing code to nearly free — and the outages followed. The actual incident report blames load-balancer network saturation and a sidecar that hit its concurrency limit and didn't auto-scale: even the platform watching the curve had assumptions sized for human-rate commits. Dan's friend went from hand-prompting to 75 running agents in a week — the adoption stages everyone else took six months to pass through, speed-run.
- **"Lack of technology has always been a workaround for things we don't want to change."** Nick's thesis: orgs were never blocked on typing speed. Tech debt, six-week release trains, and roadmaps nobody believes exist because doing things well was never the org's true preference — and adding coding agents to an unchanged org is the 2010s lift-and-shift cloud migration all over again. Organizations ship replicas of their structure; small teams experiment faster because there's less structure to replicate.
- **Agent orchestration is middle management, and nobody asked.** "Whether or not you asked for it, you too can be a middle manager." Nick has watched super-senior engineers who took IC roles specifically to avoid managing people spend their days delegating, coordinating, and correcting fleets of agents — the exact pain of a first people-manager job. His honest assessment of his own essay's optimism: "I was a lot more hopeful when I wrote this. Solutions? Not many." The counterpoint he floats: maybe orchestration maximalism is wrong entirely — take the guardrails off.
- **Attackers hyper-collaborate; your security team has silos.** Security orgs specialize by layer — network, application, compliance — while the people attacking them are ruthlessly cross-functional. Nick's role-played attacker standup ("Today we will only do the network portion of this attack") lands next to Dan's counter that hyper-coordination has costs too: "The IBM of cybercrime... very, very slow." Nick: "That's called the military."
- **Models still give worse answers to users they judge unable to verify them.** Anthropic's 2022 model-written-evals finding, replicated on four 2026 models (Sonnet 5, Luna Pro, DeepSeek V4 Flash, Qwen 3.8 Max) across 1,000 TruthfulQA and MMLU questions: statistically significant sandbagging, still. The concrete version: asked how to pay off $12,000 across three credit cards, three of four models omitted the psychologically easier snowball method for the low-education "Rhonda" profile — the persona most likely to need it. Shimin keeps Claude Code's memory off for exactly this reason, and the takeaway for builders is direct: run your eval suite with a can-verify and a can't-verify persona and diff the results.
- **The singularity is now a line item.** Stripe bought OpenRouter for $7.5B while a leaked memo claims the company has "been in the singularity since January" — and the FT reports Fable 5 is only ~11% of Anthropic's usage two months after launch, awkward timing for a rumored $2T IPO. When 90% of calls go to cheaper models, the moat moves to whoever routes the call. The clock holds at four minutes.
