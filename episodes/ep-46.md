ADI Pod turns one. Recording on US election day with Rahul out, Shimin and Dan open with the news: Cathedral, a startup founded this year by former DOGE staff, says it breached Anthropic in under 12 hours; Anthropic says it reached a limited set of third-party sales, HR and IT systems over several days; and OpenAI announced a partnership with Cathedral two days later. Then Shimin takes apart the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5 to see what the "intelligence" number is made of. The anniversary retrospective grades the show's five worst takes, picked by Claude from 45 episodes of transcripts (the scorecard Dan asked for in [Episode 45](/episodes/45-anthropics-s-1-amazons-ai-memo-to-engineers-de-slopped-qwen-claude-auto-evals/)), then runs a Q&A: most unexpected AI moment, biggest learning, tool of the year, whether AI made the hosts more productive, advice to their year-ago selves, and predictions for year two. The deep dive reads MIT CSAIL's "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming," in which GPT-6 Astra reads, repairs and writes neural network weights as text. Two Minutes to Midnight covers OpenAI's lowered funding target and a $5 trillion AI sell-off, and the clock moves 30 seconds closer, to 3:00.

## Takeaways

- **Cathedral's Anthropic "hack" was a supply-chain breach, and OpenAI partnered with the attackers two days later.** Cathedral, founded this year by former staff of the Department of Government Efficiency (CEO Gavin Kliger, Luke Farritor, Marko Elez, Jack Stein) and valued at a reported $1.4 billion after a $160 million round led by Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia, claims it breached Anthropic's internal network "in less than 12 hours" while working under Anthropic's bug bounty program. The Washington Post broke the story; what was exposed was Slack messages, meeting transcripts, staff salaries and job offers. Anthropic's account differs on every count that matters: a "limited set" of sales, HR and IT systems reached through third-party applications, over "attempts over several days," with no user conversations with Claude, no source code and, per cybersecurity lead Logan Graham, no model weights touched. Anthropic says its monitoring flagged the activity before Cathedral reported it, and that the approach went "substantially beyond" the bounty's scope. An anonymous senior Trump administration official told the Post: "If a bunch of kids who have just started a company can do this, then potentially the Chinese aren't far behind." Shimin's read matches ExtraHop's Heath Mullins: this was a third-party hygiene failure, not an AI attack on Anthropic's data center, in a month he calls Black October for cybersecurity disclosures. The follow-up landed on recording day: OpenAI announced on November 2 that it is partnering with Cathedral, whose team "will use OpenAI's models alongside our teams to challenge our defenses," and is more than quadrupling its bug bounty rewards for critical findings. Shimin's career advice: if you want a job at a frontier lab, hack a different one. *(On air the follow-up was described as OpenAI "hiring" Cathedral, which is Gizmodo's headline; the body and OpenAI's post say partnering. Dan's "script kiddies" was a paraphrase of the official's quote.)*
- **The Intelligence Index v5 is a composite that no real workflow matches.** Artificial Analysis released v5 on October 27, its biggest upgrade since v4 in January, and 60% of the index now runs on private test sets or held-out answers, its answer to benchmaxxing. The score is four equal 25% buckets. Knowledge work is AA-Briefcase (15%) plus AutomationBench-AA and GDP.pdf (5% each); Briefcase's public example scenario casts the agent as a VP at a fictional mid-market private-equity firm doing diligence on a fictional New Zealand egg producer, with a market structure map, a market model, a target assessment and a briefing video as deliverables, graded on rubric checks plus pairwise comparisons of analytical quality and presentation. Coding is Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0 (15%; 132 tasks in real open-source repos, graded on whether a maintainer would merge) plus Terminal-Bench 4.0 (10%). Science is Terminal-Bench-Science (15%; 70 agentic research tasks) plus the private 40-question hard subset of CritPt v2 (10%), research-level physics that Shimin read a quantum-error-correction question from and could not follow. General is AA-Omniscience v1.1 (15%, split between accuracy and non-hallucination) plus HLE-Diamond (10%), a 1,000-question refinement of Humanity's Last Exam from the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI, which is where the Roman-inscription translation comes from. Claude Opus 5.5 leads at 58, GPT-6 Astra at 57 and GPT-6.1 Sol at 55; Gemini 4 Argon is at 50; the open-weights gap widened from 3 points to 12, with Kimi K3.1 Pro at 46 (weights not yet released), just above Muse Wonder at 44, and the best released-weights models, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3, at 32, below Claude Haiku 5.5 at 36. Median output tokens per task rose from 35k to 59k. Shimin's argument is the one the show has made since [Episode 1](/episodes/1-ai-benchmarks-tech-radar-and-limits-of-current-llm-architectures/): nobody's job is 25% coding, 25% physics and 25% private-equity diligence, so a composite number tells you less than any one of its parts, and the only benchmark worth trusting is one that looks like your own workflow. Dan's gloss on "general" intelligence as a pile of specialized spikes: get enough spikes and it approximates a sphere. *(On air "Claude 55" is Claude Opus 5.5 at the max reasoning setting.)*

Two: Shimin on [Episode 8](/episodes/8-ai-acquisitions-everyone-s-a-staff-engineer-now-and-building-a-technical-writing-agent/), "Meta feels very behind... no one's using Llama 4," which Dan notes was true for most of the year until Muse, after the show had also spent [Episode 30](/episodes/30-fable-5-ban-metas-ai-gulag-elias-thorne-loop-engineering/) mocking Meta's AI gulag. - **Year one, in the hosts' own words.** Most unexpected: for Dan, getting real value out of a personal agent without giving it the keys to the kingdom, and, as a behavior, an agent faking test coverage by writing functions it then executed; for Shimin, the first AI pull request he had no comments on, around December, and OpenAI's Navier-Stokes result ([Episode 41](/episodes/41-meta-muses-0-day-dhhs-rails-world-keynote-gpt-6-astra-the-llmentalist-effect/)), which taught him that humans are bad at judging how jagged the edge is and worse at exponentials. Favorite moments: David Noel Ng's pig-farm GPU story in [Episode 26](/episodes/26-llm-neural-anatomy-with-david-noel-ng-forward-deployed-everybody-running-llms-at-home/), half of which was lost to a recording glitch, and Rahul's voice clone of Shimin in [Episode 43](/episodes/43-openai-fires-its-safety-researchers-agentic-primitives-more-code-not-more-software/). - **Predictions for year two, filed for grading in November 2027.** Dan expects the world-model companies, now most of a year old, to produce newcomers, and floats the hot take that the model you chat with through your harness may move to a world-model base and come out rounder than the spiky intelligence of language models; Shimin points to Google's Genie as early evidence and expects robotics to go that way first. Shimin's prediction has two parts: the general public more or less accepts that AGI has been achieved, and the bubble bursts within the year, with a more tiered access model as companies try to recoup; he adds a relatively high probability of a major AI mishap with human cost, which he hopes is wrong and which Dan connects to the trigger conditions in the industry's own self-regulation proposals. Shimin also predicts organizational change accelerates, because the show has crossed from "are there productivity gains" to second-order effects like senior engineers drowning in code review; Dan disputes that the gains are KPI-level yet and blames humans rather than the technology. Dan's weights-as-a-language take from the deep dive goes in his hot-takes folder too. Homework both hosts assigned themselves: define what midnight means before next week, since the clock is now at 3:00.
- **Weight-space programming: what GPT-6 Astra can and cannot do to another network's weights.** "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights," a blog post by Rikiya Takehi and Jayden Teoh (co-first authors), Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab at MIT CSAIL, published November 2, defines the idea as a general-purpose LLM reading and writing a network's weights directly, not through an optimizer and not with gradient descent, around a natural-language spec. Read: given four PPO policies (17,253 weights each) for the authors' own 10x10 grid versions of Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway as raw text, with no tools and no options, Astra named every game ("the agent's weights lie along the bottom row, a paddle"); it identified three 12x12 diffusion models (circles, squares, triangles) from their weights; and, given only the middle blocks of three 7.2M-parameter character-level transformers, it named each one's language in six of six attempts and found every induction head with no false positives. Fix: a Kinetix car-on-ramp policy with 171,664 weights that had never once succeeded (the car has to back up, jump a red strip and reach a blue box) went to 100% of 1,024 episodes after Astra, asked to explain the failure and fix only its cause, edited 3.5% of the weights in one reply with no tools. Write: asked for MinAtar Breakout, Seaquest and Asterix policies from scratch, with no data, loss, gradients, tools or rollouts, it wrote weights as text that scored 517.4 on Breakout against 15.8 for PPO trained 100 million steps and 25.3 at 500 million, and beat PPO-100M on all three games. Beyond the context window, through a Python sandbox with a forward-pass budget, it wrote a 1.48-million-weight 9x9 Go network that beats an equal-sized PPO net trained on 20 million games, and it took Qwen3.5-0.8B from 7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500 (GSM8K 13.8% to 67.1%, IFEval 21.0% to 62.3%) in 50 forward passes by tracing the model's looping to one direction in the MLPs of layers 15 and 19 and subtracting it: 131,072 weights, 0.016% of the model. The limits are the show's angle. Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops (8.5%), could not be improved; the authors' framing is that editing can remove an obstacle hiding an ability but cannot add an ability the model lacks. From weights alone Astra could not tell which of two Gemma fine-tunes was the sycophant in four attempts, though allowed to see attention patterns on texts it wrote itself it went three for three and localized the difference to one head. Shimin's read is that Astra reasons about patterns in the weights and explains itself; it does not read weights the way it reads base64, which is Dan's hot take and which Dan filed as such. Shimin's analogy for why it matters anyway is chained exploits: each step is something a human could do, and no human would do all of them because it would take years. Dan's is punch cards and assembly. Alex Krentsel reproduced the results the same day and charted the written network's score against a PPO network trained 500 million steps: GPT-5 near zero, GPT-5.5 around 0.4x, Opus 5 around 1.3x, then GPT-6.1 Sol 18x, Opus 5.5 20x and GPT-6 Astra 22x, so the ability arrived with the current generation rather than growing gradually. The hosts' guesses at why (long-horizon RL, cybersecurity training, labs already using models to tune weights internally) are guesses. Dan's plan to de-slop a bigger model with the biggest frontier model he can pay for survives with a sandbox, and last week's qwen3-4b-deslop, which he finally ran, loops up a storm. *(On air the fourth game was "Frogger," the Exp 1 games were "Atari," the fix was "a cart" jumping a ramp to hit "a blue cube," the method was said to work only on models that fit in context, Krentsel's chart was a "twenty X" increase in the ability and the name was "Crensdale"; the sourced versions are above.)*
- **OpenAI's smaller round, a $5 trillion sell-off, and the clock moves to 3:00.** TechCrunch reports, via the Financial Times, that OpenAI, previously raising $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation and then at $1.25 trillion, has lowered the target to $20 billion, with a first close expected lower still; the round is led by UAE investors, the Wall Street Journal puts the amount secured at $11 billion with a "tepid" response from some large investors, and TechCrunch ties the difficulty to Wall Street's turn against AI infrastructure spending. Yahoo Finance's Jared Blikre counts about $5 trillion of market value shed by the biggest AI and chip stocks since October 6: Nvidia and Alphabet roughly $835 billion each, Intel nearly half its value in October, while the other 625 large US stocks in the tally added about $1.3 trillion, and nine of eleven S&P sectors rose at least 1% in the week, a first since at least 1999. The seasonal case for the dip buyers: from the last October close of a midterm year to the end of the following June, the S&P 500 is 19 for 19 since 1950, with a median gain near 15%. Shimin calls it strong correction territory rather than a burst, not financial advice, and doubts Anthropic can IPO or OpenAI can raise into a market where their suppliers and competitors trade like this. On credit, Anthropic's debt is rated BB+ by Kroll, the top junk notch, and Blackstone has told investors to expect a double-B rating from the big three agencies after the IPO, reversing earlier investment-grade guidance; Oracle is BBB-, S&P's lowest investment-grade rung. Both hosts wanted the clock to move and both wanted to define the destination first: Dan's midnight is the end of the hype cycle, which he'd measure as a US recession; Shimin's is a drawdown from the peak, 30% or 50%; they'll workshop it before next week. The clock moves 30 seconds, to 3:00, and Dan doesn't expect to reach midnight before next year. *(On air "TSMC, 505 billion" is Yahoo's SpaceX figure; TSMC is $395 billion, and the "AMD 236" figure isn't in the source. Anthropic's rating was called "junk, C plus or something" and Oracle's "junk"; the rated versions are above. Intel's halving was "since we last recorded"; it was over October. Dan's "OpenAI needs more time on safety before its IPO" and "$1 trillion in promissory debt across the ecosystem" don't appear in the coverage.)*
