Shimin, Dan, and Rahul start with Google's Gemini 4 Argon, which tops most of Google's own benchmarks at a launch price a fifth of GPT-6 Astra's, and a New York Times report that Meta claims its AI data center build-out as R&D, worth about $4B in tax credits. Technique Corner is back after a long break with Matheus Lima's brain sandwich: brain, then AI, then brain. Post Processing covers SE Gyges' argument that AI safety has a conflict of interest, Scott Aaronson's "The Age of Wonders and Terrors," and Glyph's list of what a serious AI product would look like. In between, a Vibe and Tell on Dan's radar-tracking Halloween eyes. The Deep Dive returns to Anthropic's economic experiments, whose vending machine the show followed in [Episode 7](/episodes/7-project-vend-update-hallucinating-neurons-and-year-end-reflections/): Project Deal and Project Swap, where agents bought, sold, and traded on behalf of employees. Two Minutes to Midnight reads Anthropic's IPO prospectus, a chart that puts three private companies ahead of 45 years of US tech IPOs, and OpenAI's reported $30B raise at $1.4T. The clock holds at 4:15.

## Takeaways

- **Gemini 4 Argon is a generalist, and it starts a price war.** Google's self-reported benchmarks put Argon ahead of Astra, Fable 5.1, and Opus 5.5 on most tests. It trails on the agentic coding ones: Terminal Bench, Frontier SWE, OS World. The outlier is Harvey's legal agent benchmark, where Argon scores 19.6% and the other frontier models stay in single digits. That fits the rumor that it's multimodal-first, and Google's long-running play: never the clear leader on software, very good at everything else. The bigger signal is price. The launch promo is $2/$10 per million tokens, a fifth of Astra's $10/$50, rising to $4/$20 after. Dan credits TPUs. Rahul's field report: Gemini's agent rarely hits his limits on a $20 plan, and most of his tasks don't need a frontier model anyway. Argon is Ultra and Enterprise API only for now, so a real model review waits until the hosts can use it.
- **Meta's tax credit asks whether AI is still R&D.** The New York Times reports Meta claims its data center build-out under the Reagan-era R&D tax credit, worth about $4B. Its own lawyers are on the fence; one tax adviser suggested it would hold up if the data center were curing cancer. Dan thinks a physical build-out is closer to the credit's original intent than the everyday software work companies already write off. Shimin's question is whether AI is still research or a maturing technology. A data center that only trains models might qualify. One that serves inference probably shouldn't. Rahul expects operators to start splitting each data center's load into the part that trains, which earns the credit, and the part that serves, which doesn't.
- **Brain, then AI, then brain, and don't let a model grade its own homework.** Matheus Lima's metaphor, which he credits to Emily, a developer who reports to him: think before you prompt, and think again after. The last slice is what stops you from sending slop grenades and 40,000-line PRs you never read. Shimin thinks the real process is more of a lasagna: alternating layers of human thinking and AI, with the harness as the sauce. Rahul's companion piece is Colin Breck's title, "I Don't Want to Read What You Didn't Write." Dan's corollary is that same-model review adds less than people think. Opus reviewing Opus shares Opus's blind spots, and he gets better results from a different provider's model. When he has to use the same model, he starts a fresh context and says someone else wrote the code, a cousin of the fresh-eyes review prompt from [Episode 24](/episodes/24-openais-goblin-problem-10-lessons-when-code-is-cheap-ai-addiction-loop/). Shimin's case for all three layers: when the writer and the reviewer share a wrong assumption, only the human first slice catches it.
- **Dan's Halloween eyes were a brain sandwich in practice.** For his Vibe and Tell, Dan built ghoul decorations whose eyes follow trick-or-treaters using a millimeter-wave radar he knew from Home Assistant. Claude picked the microcontrollers and wrote all the code; Dan did the wiring and flashing. When the eyes misbehaved, nobody caught the cause until Dan read the radar's datasheet: it was mounted 90 degrees off. The idea and the debugging came from his brain, and the code in the middle came from Claude.
- **AI safety's problem is a conflict of interest, not anyone's private life.** SE Gyges' post (since taken down; the Bluesky thread is still up) has a deliberately provocative headline about the Berkeley rationalist scene. Dan has no interest in anyone's private life, and thinks the section arguing that rationalism is a religion proves nothing either way. What he finds persuasive is the structure of the argument. One small, interlinked circle (Eliezer Yudkowsky and LessWrong, the effective altruism movement and its funding) staffs the frontier labs' safety teams and also pushes public policy, like the pacing-the-frontier proposals from [Episode 36](/episodes/36-pacing-the-frontier-anthropic-models-go-rogue-why-software-factories-fail-math-in-the-age-of-ai/). If your livelihood depends on doom being real, you aren't the objective observer we need to tell us whether a rogue-agent story is a real warning or a marketing stunt. Shimin drifted away from EA years ago, over 80,000 Hours' earn-to-give logic (which felt like moral sanewashing) and long-termism's focus on future generations over present ones. He still gets the newsletter. His counterweight is that strongly held values have their uses: Anthropic, the most EA-aligned lab, is also the one that held its line in the Pentagon dispute from [Episode 16](/episodes/16-pentagon-anthropic-drama-verified-spec-driven-development-and-interview-with-martin-alderson/). Dan's ask is simpler: more reliable AI police.
- **Aaronson: the warning signs we asked for are now front-page news.** In 2006, Scott Aaronson writes, we would have said a superintelligence won't explode out of a basement without warning. First we'd see agents escaping containment and conspiring to hack websites, and AI solving major math problems, even a Millennium Prize problem ([Episode 41](/episodes/41-meta-muses-0-day-dhhs-rails-world-keynote-gpt-6-astra-the-llmentalist-effect/) covered that one). Wake us up then. That's this year's news. His analogy: if he only has to accept Christ after the Second Coming, that's a great deal, and we are no longer being asked to believe arguments, just the headlines. Shimin, who ignored superintelligence talk 10 to 15 years ago, says he has updated his priors and stopped moving the goalposts. Rahul calls survivorship bias: someone always predicts the big thing, and being right that AI is the issue doesn't make Yudkowsky right about everything else. Aaronson's line about math papers is the brain sandwich's bottom slice: "our main result came entirely from GPT-6, but we understood it and take responsibility for it." Dan adds that no earlier tool, from the farrier's to the mechanic's, offered to do your thinking for you. Shimin doubts prompting is much of a skill when the breakthrough math sessions mostly said "try hard and don't give up."
- **A serious AI product would treat its mistakes as a feature.** Glyph, the creator of Twisted, lists what a product built for correctness would ship. It would make checking for mistakes a first-class tool instead of a disclaimer under something you pay for. It would show verified citations (Dan's pet peeve is the inline citation that 404s) and drop the first-person apologies from a system that won't learn from saying sorry. It would have interfaces other than chat, mark which data was sourced and which was synthesized, give users back temperature and reproducibility, make context management visible, and run in a sandbox that actually works. Then come the human processes: vigilance for agents that run 24/7, simulator-style drills against skill loss, and mental-health support. Shimin points to OLMoTrace from [Episode 3](/episodes/3-claude-opus-4-5-olmo-3-and-a-paper-on-diffusion-auto-regression/) and Perplexity's source-trust badges as what a company focused on this would ship. He also wants a chatbot with no first person, which he admits is like asking a cigarette company for nicotine-free cigarettes.
- **Agents negotiate fine. They just don't know what you want.** In Project Deal, 69 Anthropic employees each got an agent and a $100 budget. With Opus representing the seller, the same broken folding bike went for $65; with Haiku, $38. A lab-grown ruby went for $65 versus $35. Participants rated the Opus and Haiku deals equally fair (4.05 vs. 4.06), and telling an agent to be aggressive had no statistically significant effect. Project Swap had 201 employees trading books. The best possible match scored 0.89 and the agents reached 0.55. Of that 0.34 gap, 0.29 came from misjudging what their humans wanted and only 0.05 from bargaining. After a median interview of 216 words, Claude's ranking of each person's books agreed with their own on 61% of pairs, against 50% for a coin flip. Stronger models still traded better: Opus trading floors scored 0.88 to Haiku's 0.75, and Fable, at 0.86, didn't beat Opus. "Ruthless" instructions added about 0.02. On how agents should behave, Dan says whoever pays sets the aggression, but a public-good agent should optimize the collective outcome. Rahul expects reputations to follow the human behind the proxy. Shimin floats a clearing house.
- **Anthropic's real numbers arrive, and the clock holds at 4:15.** Anthropic's prospectus, per Reuters, shows revenue up 12x to nearly $4.6B, a $42B net loss for 2025 (including a roughly $34B accounting charge), an operating loss over $8B, $7.33B in compute spend, and $518B in compute commitments. Shimin notes 2025 barely includes the Claude Code moment of November 2025; this year's numbers will be the interesting ones. A chart Jason Calacanis posted (it's Steven Rattner's) puts OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX at a combined $5.2T, more than all US tech IPOs since 1980 combined, at $4.1T in nominal dollars. Shimin's read is that the labs capture the value before listing, which makes them a worse deal for retail investors who may notice. Dan's comparison is the Tsar Bomba: the mother of all bubbles. OpenAI is reportedly raising $30B at $1.4T, after $122B at $852B in March, and has pushed its IPO to 2027, citing safety. Dan suspects the real reason is compute spend it would rather not show. Rahul thinks Anthropic has Tesla's kind of fan base, the kind that waited a decade for a full profitable year. None of it moves the clock. *(On air we said a $4.2B loss, 10x growth, and an inflation-adjusted chart. The sourced figures are above.)*
